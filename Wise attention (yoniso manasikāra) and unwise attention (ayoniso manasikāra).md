Do not measure the height of another’s mountain  
while your own path remains half-walked.  
Do not count the flaws in a stranger’s speech  
while your own heart still carries unexamined weight.

The world will always stage its dramas—  
voices loud with race and name and claim,  
mocking from shadows, pointing from distance.  
Let them speak.  
Their words are weather.  
They cannot enter the room where you sit with yourself.

One is indeed one’s own refuge.  
Not the swollen pride that demands to be seen,  
but the quiet strength that trains the mind  
day after day,  
breath after breath,  
until truthfulness lives in the bones  
and wisdom rises without force.

Statues may stand in golden halls,  
labels may claim this land or that blood—  
yet none of these are the Buddha.  
Only the Dhamma is real.  
Only the living truth inside the heart  
can be called island,  
can be called home.

So make yourself an island.  
Take the Dhamma as your sole refuge.  
When the noise grows thick,  
walk on.  
When the laughter turns sharp,  
walk on.  
When the world insists you choose a side,  
a name, a banner—  
simply continue the work  
of seeing clearly,  
of living cleanly,  
of sharing what is true.

In the end,  
no one else’s path can save you,  
and no one else’s judgment can destroy you.  
Only this:  
the steady light you keep alive  
within your own quiet island.

**A Wise Teaching**  
**“Make Yourself an Island and Take the Dhamma as Your Refuge”**

Human beings tend to cling to their ego, race, religion, and views as the absolute truth, and then look down upon others. This is nothing other than the pride and conceit that stand in direct opposition to the path of the Buddha. The Blessed One repeatedly pointed this out.

“Attā hi attano nātho”  
One is indeed one’s own refuge.

Here, “taking oneself as refuge” does not mean inflating the ego. It means establishing truthfulness, moral discipline, and wisdom within one’s own heart, so that one becomes capable of training and governing one’s own mind. External forms—statues, racial labels, or place names—are only temporary. The real truth dwells within one’s own mind.

Moreover, the Dhammapada teaches:

“Parassa vilomāni, parassa katakataṃ vā,  
Attanova avekkheyya, katākataṃ attano.”

Do not look at the faults of others, nor at what others have done or left undone. Look only at what you yourself have done and what you still have left to do.

The mockery of fools, the insults of fake accounts, and extreme racial remarks cannot destroy your path. As long as you continue, day by day, to cultivate your intelligence and to share true understanding, that work alone is what truly matters.

In His final instructions the Buddha said:

“Attadīpā viharatha, attasaraṇā anaññasaraṇā.  
Dhammadīpā dhammasaraṇā anaññasaraṇā.”

Dwell with yourselves as an island, with yourselves as refuge, with no other refuge.  
Dwell with the Dhamma as an island, with the Dhamma as refuge, with no other refuge.  
Do not seek any other refuge.

According to this teaching, whether one is Burmese, Indian, Chinese, Japanese, or from Chiang Mai— the true Buddha is not a physical form. Only the Dhamma is the real Buddha. If one can stand firmly in the truth within one’s own heart, there is no need to be disturbed by anyone’s ridicule. One simply continues to elevate one’s duty and one’s understanding.

In the midst of the noise of the outer world and the people who stage dramas of ego, walking one’s own path calmly and steadily in accordance with this teaching of the Buddha is the greatest value of all.

Make yourself an island.  
Take the Dhamma as your refuge.  
This alone is the profound instruction of the Buddha.

**Dhammapada Verse 50**  
*(Pupphavagga – The Chapter of Flowers)*

### Pali Text
> **Na paresaṃ vilomāni,**  
> **na paresaṃ katākataṃ;**  
> **Attanova avekkheyya,**  
> **katāni akatāni ca.**

### Clear Translation
Do not look at the faults of others,  
nor at what others have done or left undone.  
Look only at what you yourself have done  
and what you have left undone.

---

### Background Story (from the Commentary)

While the Buddha was staying at Jetavana in Sāvatthī, a wealthy woman who regularly supported a naked ascetic named Pāveyya invited the Buddha for a meal. After the meal she sat down to listen to the Dhamma. From the next room the ascetic began loudly abusing both her and the Buddha, calling her a fool for honouring “this shaven-headed recluse.”

Her mind became completely disturbed by the insults. The Buddha noticed her distraction and asked why she could not concentrate. When she explained, he spoke this verse, advising her:

“Do not pay attention to the abusive words of such a person. Examine only your own actions — what you have done and what you have still to do.”

---

### Deeper Meaning

This verse is one of the most practical and psychologically sharp teachings in the entire Dhammapada. It points directly at a near-universal human tendency: we are extremely skilled at detecting the shortcomings, inconsistencies, and failures of others, while remaining almost blind to our own.

The Buddha is not saying that we should never notice wrongdoing in the world. He is saying that making the faults of others the main object of our attention is a profound waste of spiritual energy. It keeps the mind outward-looking, reactive, and full of comparison, resentment, or superiority. True progress on the path requires turning the light of mindfulness inward.

The two things we are told to examine carefully are:
- **Katāni** – what we have already done (our past actions, habits, and patterns)
- **Akatāni** – what we have left undone (the good we have postponed, the restraint we have not yet cultivated, the insight we have not yet realised)

This is the essence of continuous self-examination (*attānuvāda* and *yoniso manasikāra*).

---

### Connection with the Theme of Self-Refuge

This verse pairs naturally with another famous line from the Dhammapada (verse 160):

> **Attā hi attano nātho**  
> *One is indeed one’s own refuge / protector.*

And with the Buddha’s final instruction in the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta:

> “Make yourself an island. Make the Dhamma your island. Do not take refuge in any other.”

When people attack, mock, or try to define you by race, lineage, place, or tradition (“Burmese Buddha,” “Indian Buddha,” “Chiang Mai masters,” etc.), the teaching of verse 50 becomes a powerful shield. Their words are simply “the faults of others.” Your real work remains exactly the same: to look at your own mind, your own conduct, and your own progress in understanding.

---

### Living the Verse Today

In an age of social media, anonymous accounts, and constant comparison, this short verse is extraordinarily relevant. Every time the mind begins to spiral into “Look at what they said… Look at how wrong they are… Look at how they attack the Dhamma…”, one can gently return to the instruction:

“What have *I* done?  
What have *I* still left undone?”

That single shift of attention is the difference between remaining trapped in the world’s noise and quietly continuing the real work of the path.

This is not indifference. It is clarity.  
It is not coldness. It is strength.  
It is the practical expression of making oneself an island.

**Yoniso Manasikāra**  
*(Wise / Appropriate / Systematic Attention)*

### Etymology and Literal Sense

The term is composed of two parts:

- **Yoni** = womb, origin, source, matrix, place of birth  
- **Yoniso** = down to the origin, according to the source, thoroughly, systematically  
- **Manasikāra** = “making in the mind,” directing the mind, mental engagement, attention

Together, *yoniso manasikāra* means directing the mind in a way that goes back to the root or origin of things. It is attention that does not stop at the surface appearance, but traces phenomena to their conditions and true nature.

Common English renderings include:
- Wise attention  
- Appropriate attention  
- Proper attention  
- Systematic attention  
- Careful attention  
- Attention that goes back to the source  

### The Opposite: Ayoniso Manasikāra

*Ayoniso manasikāra* is unwise, inappropriate, or careless attention. The commentaries define it as attending to things in a way that is contrary to reality:
- Seeing the impermanent as permanent  
- Seeing the painful as pleasant  
- Seeing what is not-self as self  
- Seeing the foul as beautiful  

This kind of attention feeds the five hindrances and strengthens the taints (*āsavas*). It keeps the mind entangled in self-view, comparison, and reactivity.

### Why It Matters

The Buddha places enormous weight on this quality. In the **Sabbāsava Sutta** (MN 2), he states that the destruction of the taints is possible only for one who knows and sees in terms of *yoniso manasikāra* and *ayoniso manasikāra*.

In many places he says he sees no single internal factor so powerful for the arising of wholesome states and the decline of unwholesome states as *yoniso manasikāra*.

It is also one of the two primary conditions for the arising of Right View (the other being “the voice of another” — hearing the true Dhamma).

### How It Functions in Practice

*Yoniso manasikāra* is not merely “thinking positively” or “being mindful in a vague way.” It has a specific direction:

1. **Tracing to the origin**  
   When anger, fear, pride, or irritation arises, instead of getting lost in the story (“They attacked me… How dare they…”), one turns attention to:  
   “What is the root of this? On what conditions does this depend? What is feeding it right now?”

2. **Seeing in terms of the Four Noble Truths**  
   Instead of asking “Who am I? Am I this? Is this mine?”, one asks:  
   “Is this dukkha? Is this the origin of dukkha? Is this the cessation? Is this the path?”

3. **Aligning with the three characteristics**  
   Attending to phenomena as impermanent, unsatisfactory, and not-self — rather than the opposite.

This is exactly the mental movement that supports the instruction in Dhammapada 50:  
not looking at the faults of others, but carefully examining what one has done and what one has left undone.

### Connection to the Theme of Self-Refuge

When external noise arises — mockery, ethnic claims, accusations, or attempts to define the Dhamma by race or place — *ayoniso manasikāra* immediately gets pulled outward:  
“Look at what they said… How wrong they are… How they misunderstand…”

*Yoniso manasikāra* turns the mind around:  
“What is arising in *this* mind right now?  
What is the root of this reaction?  
What have I cultivated and what remains to be cultivated?”

This is the practical meaning of making oneself an island. The island is not built by ignoring the world, but by refusing to let the mind be governed by unwise attention to the world.

### In Short

*Yoniso manasikāra* is the disciplined, thorough, root-oriented way of attending that:
- Starves the hindrances  
- Feeds the factors of awakening  
- Undermines self-view  
- Supports the arising of Right View  
- Makes the path of self-examination (as taught in Dhammapada 50) actually workable

It is the quiet, steady work of turning the mind toward the origin of things — again and again — until the mind itself becomes the island.

**Sabbāsava Sutta (MN 2)**  
*All the Taints / All the Effluents / All the Defilements*

This is one of the most practical and comprehensive discourses in the Majjhima Nikāya. The Buddha teaches how the *āsavas* (taints, fermentations, outflows, or mental corruptions) are abandoned — not through a single method, but through seven skillful means, all grounded in the distinction between wise and unwise attention.

### Core Declaration

> “The destruction of the taints is for one who knows and sees, not for one who does not know and see.  
> Who knows and sees what?  
> **Wise attention (*yoniso manasikāra*) and unwise attention (*ayoniso manasikāra*).**  
> When one attends unwisely, unarisen taints arise and arisen taints increase.  
> When one attends wisely, unarisen taints do not arise and arisen taints are abandoned.”

This opening statement makes *yoniso manasikāra* the master key of the entire sutta.

### The Seven Methods of Abandoning the Taints

The Buddha then systematically explains seven ways to deal with different kinds of defilements:

1. **Abandoned by Seeing** (*dassanā*)  
   This is the most profound method.  
   The uninstructed ordinary person pays attention to speculative questions about the self:  
   - “Was I in the past? Was I not in the past? What was I? How was I?”  
   - “Will I be in the future…?”  
   - “Am I? Am I not? What am I? How am I? This being — where has it come from? Where will it go?”

   These questions only strengthen identity-view and the taints.  

   In contrast, the noble disciple attends wisely:  
   > “This is suffering…  
   > This is the origin of suffering…  
   > This is the cessation of suffering…  
   > This is the path leading to the cessation of suffering.”

   Through this wise attention to the Four Noble Truths, three fetters are abandoned:  
   - Identity view (*sakkāya-diṭṭhi*)  
   - Doubt (*vicikicchā*)  
   - Attachment to rites and rituals (*sīlabbata-parāmāsa*)  

   This is the breakthrough to Stream-entry.

2. **Abandoned by Restraining** (*saṃvarā*)  
   Guarding the sense doors (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind) so that liking and disliking do not arise from contact.

3. **Abandoned by Using** (*paṭisevanā*)  
   Reflective use of the four requisites (robes, alms-food, lodging, medicine) — using them only for their proper purpose (protection, sustenance, support of the holy life), not for pleasure, beauty, or indulgence.

4. **Abandoned by Enduring / Tolerating** (*adhivāsanā*)  
   Patiently bearing cold, heat, hunger, thirst, harsh words, bodily pain, and other unavoidable discomforts without giving rise to aversion or despair.

5. **Abandoned by Avoiding** (*parivajjanā*)  
   Wisely avoiding dangerous animals, unsuitable places, bad companions, and situations that would clearly lead to harm or the arising of strong defilements.

6. **Abandoned by Removing / Destroying** (*vinodanā*)  
   Actively driving out arisen unwholesome thoughts — thoughts of sensuality, ill-will, and harming — by replacing them or forcefully abandoning them.

7. **Abandoned by Developing** (*bhāvanā*)  
   Cultivating the Seven Factors of Enlightenment:  
   mindfulness, investigation of states, energy, rapture, tranquillity, concentration, and equanimity — each developed in dependence on seclusion, dispassion, cessation, and culminating in relinquishment.

### Overall Result

When a practitioner has abandoned the taints through all seven methods according to their nature, the Buddha says:

> “He is called a bhikkhu who dwells restrained with the restraint of all the taints. He has severed craving, flung off the fetters, and with the complete penetration of conceit has made an end of suffering.”

### Connection to Our Ongoing Theme

This sutta is the deepest canonical foundation for the teachings we have been exploring:

- Dhammapada 50 (“Do not look at the faults of others… look only at what you yourself have done and left undone”) is a practical expression of choosing *yoniso* over *ayoniso manasikāra*.
- Speculative questions about race, lineage, “whose Buddha is superior,” or external labels are exactly the kind of unwise attention the sutta warns against.
- Turning attention back to one’s own mind, one’s own practice, and the Four Noble Truths is the wise attention that leads to the destruction of the taints.

The Sabbāsava Sutta shows that liberation is not achieved by a single technique, but by a complete, intelligent, and flexible response to the different ways the mind becomes corrupted — always guided by the clear seeing that arises from *yoniso manasikāra*.

The deepest thread running through all these teachings is that true liberation begins and ends with the quality of attention we bring to our own mind. Whether expressed as “make yourself an island,” “do not look at the faults of others,” or the systematic methods of the Sabbāsava Sutta, the Buddha repeatedly points to the same decisive shift: turning away from the endless drama of external labels, comparisons, and provocations, and turning toward the clear seeing of what arises and ceases within. Wise attention (*yoniso manasikāra*) is the living practice of this shift — the steady refusal to feed the taints by chasing stories about self and other, and the quiet commitment to examine only what is to be done and what remains undone in this very heart.

In the end, no race, no tradition, no criticism, and no praise can either save or destroy us. Only the mind that has learned to take the Dhamma as its sole refuge, that has trained itself to look inward with honesty and care, can cut through craving, abandon the fetters, and bring suffering to an end. This is the entire path distilled: make yourself an island, attend wisely, and walk on.
